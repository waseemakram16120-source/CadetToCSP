600 Marks — Your Choice

Optional Subjects — Seven Groups

Selection rules (CE Rules): your optional subjects must total exactly 600 marks. You may not take more than 200 marks from any one group, and a 100-mark subject is one paper while a 200-mark subject is two papers. Group I allows one 200-mark subject only; Group II allows one 200-mark subject or two 100-mark subjects; Groups III–VII allow one 100-mark subject each. You do not have to touch every group. Optional subjects cannot be changed after you submit your application — choose carefully, and confirm the group list in the FPSC advertisement for your year.

How to Choose (Strategy)

  • Background first: subjects you have already studied score higher and take less time.
  • Overlap wins: International Relations overlaps Current Affairs; History overlaps Pakistan Affairs; Public Administration overlaps Governance.
  • Scoring trend: check recent years' scoring trends, but never pick a subject you cannot study for three months straight.
  • Balance the load: mix one heavy (200-mark) subject with lighter 100-mark ones; avoid two cramming-heavy subjects together.

Eligibility — Check Before You Apply

Who Can Apply

  • Nationality: a citizen of Pakistan (or a person holding a valid claim under the rules).
  • Education: a bachelor's degree with at least a second division (or Grade C) from a recognised university.
  • Age: generally 21 to 30 years on the cut-off date (31 December of the year before the exam), with relaxation under the rules for specified categories (e.g. government servants, recognised tribes/areas).
  • Attempts: a maximum of three attempts — an attempt counts once you appear in any paper of the written examination.

Before You Register

  • Read the FPSC advertisement for your year — it is the only final source for dates, fee, age relaxation and group rules.
  • Pass the MPT screening test first; only MPT qualifiers can sit the written examination.
  • Choose optional subjects before applying — they cannot be changed after submission.
  • Keep your documents ready: degree, CNIC, domicile and photographs as per the advertisement.

The Essay Checklist — Before You Write a Word

✓ Outline first (20–25 min): introduction, thesis statement, 4–6 main arguments with evidence, counter-argument, conclusion.

✓ Length: about 2,500–3,000 words — too short looks thin, too long looks uncontrolled.

✓ Thesis in the introduction: one clear sentence that answers the topic directly.

✓ Argument, not summary: every paragraph must prove something, with facts, examples or quotations.

✓ Counter-argument: answer the strongest objection to your view, then refute it.

✓ Conclusion: restate the thesis and end with a way forward — no new arguments here.

✓ Language: clear, formal English; short sentences; no slang, no SMS English, no memorised paragraphs that do not fit the topic.

✓ Time check: outline 25 min → writing ~2 hours → revision 20 min → word count check 5 min.

English (Précis & Composition) — 100 Marks

Précis Practice — Say It in One-Third the Words

The Rules of a Good Précis

✓ Length: about one-third of the original passage — never much more, never much less.

✓ Own words: rewrite the sense in your own language; do not copy whole sentences from the passage.

✓ Title: give a short, suitable title that captures the central idea.

✓ No comments: no opinions, no examples of your own, no criticism — only what the author says.

✓ One thread: keep the passage's main idea and its logical flow; drop illustrations and repetitions.

✓ Third person, past tense: write the précis in the third person and past tense unless the passage demands otherwise.

Passage 1 — The Value of Reading

Original: 150 words

Reading is the cheapest and most reliable way to educate oneself. A person who reads widely travels through centuries and countries without leaving his room, meets great minds, and learns from their mistakes and triumphs. Yet in an age of short videos and instant messages, the habit of reading is fading, especially among the young. Many read only what an examination demands and stop the moment the paper is over. This is a loss, because examinations test memory while books build judgement. A reader learns to weigh arguments, to doubt claims, and to hold two opposing ideas in mind without confusion. Nations that read produce citizens who can think, and citizens who can think build institutions that last. The habit need not be grand: twenty pages a day, kept without fail, amount to more than twenty books in a year. What matters is regularity, not speed, and curiosity, not compulsion.

Show model précis — “Reading Builds Judgement” (52 words)

Reading is the cheapest way to educate oneself: books let a reader meet great minds and build judgement, while examinations test only memory. Yet the habit is fading among the young. Twenty pages a day amount to more than twenty books a year, and nations that read produce citizens who can think.

Why it works: every sentence of the original survives in compressed form; the “twenty pages” example is kept because it proves the point; no opinion of the précis-writer is added.

Passage 2 — Discipline

Original: 149 words

Discipline is often misunderstood as punishment, but it is really the habit of doing what ought to be done, whether one feels like it or not. Talent without discipline is like a river without banks: it spreads widely and reaches nowhere. The student who studies a little every day will, in a year, overtake the brilliant student who studies only before examinations. Armies understand this better than most institutions, which is why their training repeats small acts until they become reflex. The same principle governs civil service: files move on time, promises are kept, and the public is served, only when officers act from habit rather than mood. Discipline also protects freedom, because a person who governs himself does not need to be governed by others. It is not the enemy of creativity; it is the frame that holds creativity steady long enough for it to produce something lasting.

Show model précis — “Discipline: Habit, Not Punishment” (52 words)

Discipline is the habit of doing what ought to be done, regardless of mood, not punishment. Talent without it spreads everywhere and reaches nowhere, while steady daily study overtakes last-minute brilliance. Armies build it through repetition, and the civil service depends on it for timely work. Self-governed people need no external control.

Why it works: the river image is dropped (illustration, not argument), the freedom point is kept in one line, and the whole précis follows the original's order.

Passage 3 — Water in Pakistan

Original: 143 words

Water is the quiet foundation of Pakistan's economy, yet it is treated as if it were limitless. Four out of five Pakistanis depend, directly or indirectly, on farming, and farming depends on the Indus and its canals. But the canals lose much of their water to seepage and theft, the water table falls every year, and the population grows faster than the supply. Climate change has made the pattern worse: glaciers melt faster, rains fall in violent bursts, and droughts last longer. The answer is not a single dam but a discipline of use: lining canals, pricing water honestly, growing crops that suit dry lands, and storing monsoon rain instead of letting it run to the sea. Countries that learned this lesson early now export food; countries that ignored it import it. Pakistan must choose which of the two it wishes to be.

Show model précis — “Pakistan's Water Challenge” (52 words)

Water underpins Pakistan's farm economy, yet it is wasted through canal seepage, theft, and falling water tables, while population growth and climate change worsen scarcity. The remedy is disciplined use: lined canals, honest pricing, dry-land crops, and rain storage. Nations that learned this export food; Pakistan must decide whether to join them.

Why it works: the figures (four out of five) are dropped as detail, the chain of causes is kept, and the closing choice is preserved as the author's conclusion.

Try it yourself: cover the model précis, write your own in about 50 words, then compare — did you keep the main idea, drop the illustrations, and stay in your own words?

300 Marks • 100 to Pass

Interview — Psychological Assessment & Viva Voce

Psychological Assessment — Qualifying Only

Candidates who clear the written examination face psychological testing before the interview. It carries no marks in the final merit — it is a pass/fail judgement of suitability.

  • Written tests: sentence completion, word association, picture stories and similar exercises — answer honestly and consistently; the tests are designed to catch rehearsed answers.
  • Group tasks: group discussion and command tasks that show leadership, listening and teamwork under pressure.
  • Individual assessment: a short interview with a psychologist about your background, attitudes and motivation.
  • What they look for: emotional stability, integrity, decision-making, social adaptability and a genuine service motive.

Viva Voce — 300 Marks

A formal interview before an FPSC panel, usually lasting 20–30 minutes. You need 100 marks (33%) to pass, and the score counts directly in your final merit position — a strong viva can lift you many places up the list.

  • What is tested: communication, confidence, awareness of current affairs, grip on your optional subjects, and suitability for public service.
  • Your file is on the table: the panel has your written marks, psychological report and service preferences — expect questions from all of them.
  • Honesty beats performance: say “I don't know” rather than guessing; panels respect it and penalise bluffing.

20 Common Interview Questions — With Answer Guidance

Tap a question to see how to approach it. These are the questions panels ask year after year.

1. Introduce yourself.

Keep it to 60–90 seconds: name, education, domicile, optional subjects, and one line on why you chose the civil service. Do not recite your whole life story — the panel has your form in front of them.

2. Why do you want to join the civil service?

Lead with service, not status: public service, solving real problems, and using your education for the country. Avoid “power”, “prestige” or “job security” as your first reasons.

3. Why did you choose your optional subjects?

Link each subject to your academic background, genuine interest or a scoring record — and be ready for a follow-up question from any of them. Never say “someone told me it scores well”.

4. Why is this service group your first preference?

Know what the group actually does day to day (PAS, PSP, FSP, Customs, IRS and the rest) and match it to your strengths. A vague “I like administration” is not an answer.

5. What is the biggest problem facing Pakistan today?

Pick ONE problem and structure it: what it is, why it exists, what it costs, and what you would do about it. A list of ten problems with no depth scores nothing.

6. What do you know about the 18th Amendment?

Provincial autonomy, devolution of powers, the Council of Common Interests and Article 25-A. Be ready for a follow-up: has devolution worked, and where has it failed?

7. Why does Pakistan keep going to the IMF?

Explain the balance-of-payments problem in plain words: low exports, low tax collection, high imports and debt. Know the current programme (Extended Fund Facility) and its main conditions.

8. CPEC — opportunities and concerns?

Balance both sides: energy, infrastructure and Gwadar on one side; debt, transparency and provincial shares on the other. Take a position and defend it with facts.

9. Climate change and Pakistan — what should we do?

Reference the 2022 floods, Pakistan's tiny share of global emissions, and the case for climate finance, adaptation and water management. This is a favourite panel topic.

10. What does a Deputy Commissioner / Assistant Commissioner actually do?

Revenue, law and order, price control, coordination of departments, and public dealing. If PAS is your preference, you must know this job in detail.

11. Parliamentary vs presidential system — which suits Pakistan?

Define both, then argue from Pakistan's own history (1973 Constitution, parliamentary tradition) rather than from theory alone. Expect a counter-question.

12. What are your strengths and weaknesses?

Give real strengths with examples, and a real weakness with what you are doing about it. “I am a perfectionist” is not a weakness — panels have heard it a thousand times.

13. What newspapers and books do you read?

Answer honestly — and actually read what you name. The panel will ask your opinion on something from today's paper or from a book you claim to have read.

14. Tell us about your hometown / district.

Its history, economy, famous people and main problems — plus one practical suggestion to improve it. Every candidate is asked this; few prepare it.

15. What is the role of social media in society today?

Balance: information and accountability on one side, misinformation and polarisation on the other. Give a concrete example, not a slogan.

16. Who is your role model and why?

Choose someone whose values connect to public service and explain the connection in two sentences. Avoid naming a controversial political figure without a careful reason.

17. What would you do if a senior asked you to do something unlawful?

The correct frame: rules first, polite refusal, and the proper channel (written orders, reporting up the chain). Never say you would obey, and never sound self-righteous about it.

18. What are Pakistan's main foreign policy challenges?

Structure by neighbour and partner: India, Afghanistan, the US, China, the Gulf states — one line on each, with the current state of the relationship.

19. What is good governance?

Transparency, accountability, rule of law, participation and effective service delivery — then one Pakistani example where it is missing and one where it works.

20. Where do you see yourself in ten years?

Service-focused and realistic: a mid-career officer who has delivered in the field, not a fantasy of a top post. Show ambition tied to work, not to rank.

Practical Tips for Interview Day

✓ Dress: formal, neat and simple — a suit or shalwar kameez with waistcoat; polished shoes; nothing flashy.

✓ Arrive early: reach the centre at least 45 minutes before your slot, documents in order.

✓ Current affairs: read the newspaper every morning for the last two weeks before the interview; know the top five national and five international stories.

✓ Optional-subject grip: revise your optionals the week before — the panel will test them.

✓ Body language: sit straight, make eye contact, listen to the full question before answering, and do not interrupt.

✓ Honesty: “I don't know, but I would find out” beats a confident wrong answer every time.

✓ Mock interviews: practise at least three full mock interviews with someone who will ask hard follow-ups.

✓ Stay calm: the panel is not your enemy — a steady, respectful tone matters as much as the content.
